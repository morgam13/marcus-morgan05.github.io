NYC's RoofScape

Anatomy of the Skin

01 · The idea

Above its streets lies the city's largest untapped resource: its roofscape.

New York City is an ever-growing experiment in the condition of congestion, proving an interdependent covariation between ecological, economic and social deprivation. It is a city that grows up, not out. Its roofscape is a landscape ready to be covered in an ecological, economic and social skin.

The pressures

A rising heat index. Falling air quality. Food prices and food insecurity. Too little park and social space. Sewers that overflow when it rains. Electricity still made from fossil fuel.

The answer

Green, blue and photovoltaic systems, placed on the one surface the city has left: the roof. Today a roof either holds equipment or empty space.

The question

What value can these systems return ecologically, economically, and socially, and how is that value made attractive to the building owners?

Three systems · what it returns

Two block typologies

The block decides the intervention.

The same systems land differently depending on the block typology, and the typology comes from how the block is built. Each typology is more common in some parts of the city than others, so a geographic pattern can be seen, but the typology does not depend on geography.

The stakeholders · who holds the control

Building owners

The main stakeholders. They own the roofs, so they decide whether anything is built. The study shows what each system returns to them.

Policymakers

The stakeholders with direct influence over owners. They write the regulations, requirements and incentives that make the change happen. Current local laws that show this are Local Laws 92, 94 and 97, the privately owned public space rules and the urban agriculture laws.

The people of the city

The roofscape is an untapped landscape for them. Residents, tenants and neighbours receive the cooler air, the safer streets, the food and the space.

02 · The city today

What a bare roofscape costs.

About 40,000 acres of rooftop cover the five boroughs. Almost none of it is planted, holds water or makes power. The cost shows up ecologically, economically and socially, and each cost feeds the others.

Two real city blocks, analysed as case studies

The citywide picture, measured roof by roof.

Both blocks exist. Every roof on them was surveyed for this research from the city's 2024 aerial imagery.

Case study 1 · two years of change

One new photovoltaic array in two years.

Between the city's 2022 and 2024 flights over Block 823, the only roof that changed use is lot 52, which gained a photovoltaic array. Newer Google Earth imagery shows two more arrays on lot 75. At that pace the block's 74 roofs would take generations to activate.

04 · Hollow core block

Block 823: share the middle, activate the roofs.

Sunset Park, 54th to 55th Street between 4th and 5th Avenue. Rowhouses ring the block and 73 fenced backyards fill its core. The proposal joins a slice of every yard into one sunken sponge courtyard.

Intervention 1 · the courtyard

How much backyard does each owner give?

Shares of 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50% were studied. 30% performed best, but any share is feasible. It depends on the outcome the block wants.

The program behind it

A city program the block chooses to join.

The Sponge Courtyard Program is a step-by-step path the city offers toward a more resilient block. Nothing is imposed. These are privately owned yards, so the city's role is to make joining easy and attractive, with incentives, design help and one place to see what the block gains. Owners organise like a homeowners association, and the block joins only if every owner votes yes.

Block 787: open the roofs to the public.

Garment District, West 37th to West 38th Street between 7th and 8th Avenue. Lofts built wall to wall, with little to no interior open space. Here the intervention is private: each owner builds, and the city pays them to let the public up.

The proposal

Rooftop POPS: privately owned public roofscapes.

Modelled on the city's Privately Owned Public Space program. Access uses what code already requires: the exit stair discharges to the street, separate from the tenant lobby, so it becomes the public way up. A dedicated lift beside it makes the roof accessible.

Against the costs of a bare roofscape

Ecological

A lower heat index. Cleaner air. Rain captured, redirected and reused, so less sewage overflows. Habitat for birds and pollinators. Clean power in place of fossil fuel.

Economic

Lower energy, cooling and water bills. A roof membrane that lasts longer. Tax abatements claimed and Local Law 97 fines avoided. Flood damage that never happens. Income from produce, rental, tickets and power.

Social

Fewer heat deaths. Food grown where it is needed. Safer homes in a storm. Shared energy. Gardens, parks and gathering space in a city that has run out of ground.
